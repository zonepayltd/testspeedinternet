To boost your WiFi signal, start with router placement: move it to a central, raised, open spot away from thick walls, metal, and appliances. Then reduce interference by using the 5 GHz band and a less crowded channel, keep the firmware updated, and, if some rooms are still weak, add a mesh node or a wired access point. Most homes see a real improvement from the free fixes alone.

**Key takeaways**

- **Placement first:** central, elevated, and out in the open beats any gadget.
- **Use the right band:** 5 GHz (or 6 GHz) for speed nearby, 2.4 GHz for range.
- **Cut interference:** change channels, move away from microwaves and cordless phones.
- **Update and restart:** firmware updates fix bugs and can improve performance.
- **Add coverage smartly:** mesh or wired access points beat cheap extenders for large homes.

## Measure your WiFi signal before you change anything

You can't tell what helped unless you know where you started. Pick three or four places where you actually use the internet, such as the couch, home office, and bedroom, and test each one.

1. Run the [WiFi speed test](/tools/wifi-speed-test) a few feet from the router to set a best-case baseline.
2. Repeat the test in each of your chosen spots, standing or sitting where you normally would.
3. Note download, upload, and ping for each location.
4. Make one change at a time, then retest the same spots.

If speeds are poor even next to the router, the issue may be your plan, modem, or provider rather than the WiFi signal. In that case, start by checking your wired speed and equipment instead.

## Router placement: the biggest free upgrade

WiFi radiates outward from the router's antennas, weakening with distance and with every obstacle it passes through. Where you put the router has more effect on coverage than almost any setting.

### Tip 1: Put the router in a central location

Routers often end up wherever the provider's cable enters the house, which is frequently a corner or an outside wall. That wastes half the signal on the outdoors. If you can, place it near the middle of your home, or at least closer to the rooms where you need the strongest signal. A longer Ethernet or coax cable is a cheap way to relocate it.

### Tip 2: Raise it off the floor

Signals travel better when they start above furniture and people. A shelf, the top of a bookcase, or a wall mount at roughly head or chest height usually works better than the floor or a low TV stand. In a two-story home, a high shelf near the middle of the ground floor, or a spot on the second floor near the stairs, often gives balanced coverage.

### Tip 3: Keep it in the open

Closets, cabinets, and media consoles block and absorb signal. So do metal objects such as filing cabinets, refrigerators, and radiators, plus mirrors and large fish tanks. Give the router clear space on all sides.

### Tip 4: Position the antennas sensibly

If your router has adjustable external antennas, try angling some vertically and some horizontally. Vertical antennas tend to spread signal across a floor, while angled or horizontal ones can help reach floors above and below. Small adjustments, followed by a retest, are the way to find what works in your home.

## How walls and materials affect WiFi

Not all obstacles are equal. Here's a rough guide to how common materials affect WiFi signals:

| Material | Effect on WiFi | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Drywall and wood | Low | Several walls still add up |
| Glass (plain) | Low to moderate | Low-E or tinted glass can block much more |
| Brick | Moderate to high | Thick, older brick walls are especially tough |
| Concrete and stone | High | Floors and basement walls often create dead zones |
| Metal (appliances, ducts, mesh) | Very high | Can reflect or block signal entirely |
| Water (fish tanks, people) | Moderate | Bodies of water absorb WiFi frequencies |

Higher frequencies such as 5 GHz and 6 GHz lose more strength passing through dense materials than 2.4 GHz does. That's why the faster bands can be strong in the same room but drop off two walls away.

## Reduce interference and congestion

Even a well-placed router can struggle if the airwaves are crowded.

### Tip 5: Use the 5 GHz or 6 GHz band where possible

The 2.4 GHz band has only a few non-overlapping channels and is shared with microwaves, Bluetooth, and many smart devices. The 5 GHz band has far more room and higher speeds, and 6 GHz (on WiFi 6E and WiFi 7 routers) is cleaner still. Use 2.4 GHz for distant rooms and low-bandwidth gadgets. Our guide to [2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz WiFi](/blog/2-4-ghz-vs-5-ghz-wifi) explains the trade-offs in detail.

### Tip 6: Choose a less crowded channel

Most routers pick channels automatically, but they don't always choose well. On 2.4 GHz, stick to channels 1, 6, or 11 because they don't overlap. Many router apps show nearby networks; pick the channel with the fewest neighbors. Restarting the router often triggers a fresh automatic scan, too.

### Tip 7: Move away from interfering devices

Microwave ovens, older cordless phones, baby monitors, and wireless video senders can disrupt 2.4 GHz WiFi. Keep the router at least a few feet away from them, and notice whether your WiFi drops when the microwave is running.

### Tip 8: Limit bandwidth hogs

Signal strength isn't the only factor in how fast WiFi feels. A device constantly uploading security camera footage or downloading game updates takes airtime from everything else. Many routers let you set QoS (Quality of Service) priorities so video calls or gaming come first.

Before moving on to hardware changes, it's worth checking your progress. [Run a free speed test](/) in your weakest room and compare it with your starting numbers.

## Keep your router up to date

### Tip 9: Update the firmware

Router firmware updates fix bugs, close security holes, and sometimes improve performance and stability. Log in to your router's app or admin page (often at an address like 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1) and check for updates. Turn on automatic updates if the option exists.

### Tip 10: Restart on a schedule, and upgrade old hardware

A restart clears memory and stuck connections; some routers let you schedule a weekly reboot overnight. If your router is many years old and only supports WiFi 4 or WiFi 5, upgrading to a WiFi 6 or newer model can improve both speed and how well it handles many devices at once. See [WiFi 6 vs WiFi 6E vs WiFi 7](/blog/wifi-6-vs-wifi-6e-vs-wifi-7) to decide which standard makes sense.

## Extend coverage to dead zones

If placement and settings changes still leave weak spots, it's time to add hardware.

### Tip 11: Choose mesh WiFi or a wired access point over a basic extender

| Option | Best for | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| WiFi extender | One small dead zone | Cheap, easy to set up | Often halves speed, may create a separate network |
| Mesh system | Medium to large homes, multiple floors | Seamless roaming, one network name, easy app setup | Costs more; wireless backhaul loses some speed |
| Wired access point | Homes with Ethernet runs | Full speed at each point, very reliable | Requires cabling |
| Powerline or MoCA adapter + access point | Homes without Ethernet | Uses existing electrical or coax wiring | Powerline performance varies with wiring |

Place a mesh node or extender where it still gets a decent signal from the router, typically about halfway to the dead zone, rather than inside the dead zone itself. For a deeper comparison, read [mesh WiFi vs WiFi extender](/blog/mesh-wifi-vs-wifi-extender).

### Tip 12: Wire what you can

Every device you plug in with Ethernet, such as a desktop PC, game console, or smart TV, frees up wireless airtime for phones and laptops. It also gives that device a faster, more stable connection. If you have a mesh system, connecting the nodes to each other with Ethernet (wired backhaul) can dramatically improve performance.

## WiFi boosting myths to skip

A few popular suggestions don't deliver much:

- **Foil or homemade reflectors:** they can redirect signal slightly, but usually weaken coverage elsewhere.
- **"WiFi booster" apps:** a phone app can't increase a router's transmit power or your device's antenna strength.
- **Maximum transmit power everywhere:** turning power up helps the router reach farther, but your phone's weaker transmitter still has to reach back. Balanced coverage matters more.
- **Buying the highest number on the box:** ratings like "AX6000" are combined theoretical totals across bands, not what one device will get.

## Bottom line

The most effective ways to boost your WiFi signal are free: move the router to a central, raised, open spot, use the 5 GHz band where you can, pick a cleaner channel, and keep the firmware updated. Only after that should you spend money, and when you do, mesh systems and wired access points usually beat basic extenders.

Make one change at a time and measure the result so you know what actually worked. When you're ready, [test your WiFi speed](/) in each room and compare it with where you started.
