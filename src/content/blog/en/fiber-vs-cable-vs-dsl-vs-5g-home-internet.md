**Fiber** is the best home internet connection for most people when it's available: it offers the fastest speeds, symmetrical upload and the lowest latency. **Cable** is a strong second, with fast downloads but much slower uploads. **5G home internet** is a flexible wireless alternative whose performance depends on signal and network load, and **DSL** is an older copper-line technology best used only when nothing else is available. The right choice comes down to what's offered at your address and how you use the internet.

**Key takeaways**

- Fiber: fastest and most consistent, with upload often equal to download.
- Cable: widely available, fast downloads, but upload is usually a fraction of download.
- 5G home internet: no wiring needed and often competitive pricing, but speed and ping vary with signal and congestion.
- DSL: slowest wired option; speed drops the farther you are from the provider's equipment.
- Always check real performance with a speed test, not just the advertised "up to" number.

## Home internet types compared at a glance

Here is a side-by-side overview. Figures are typical ranges for residential plans in the US, UK and Europe; individual plans and locations vary.

| | Fiber | Cable | 5G home internet | DSL |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How it reaches you | Light through glass fiber | Coaxial TV cable (DOCSIS) | Cellular radio to a gateway | Copper phone line |
| Typical download | 300 Mbps–2 Gbps+ | 100 Mbps–1 Gbps+ | ~50–400 Mbps | ~5–100 Mbps |
| Typical upload | Often equal to download | ~10–50 Mbps (some plans higher) | ~10–50 Mbps | ~1–20 Mbps |
| Typical latency to nearby servers | Very low | Low | Moderate, more variable | Low to moderate |
| Consistency at peak times | Excellent | Good, can dip in busy areas | Variable | Fair to good |
| Installation | Technician often required | Usually self-install | Self-install, plug in | Usually self-install |
| Availability | Growing, not universal | Very wide in towns and cities | Depends on 5G coverage | Wide but shrinking |

In the US, the FCC's benchmark for broadband is currently 100 Mbps download and 20 Mbps upload. Fiber and most cable plans meet that easily; many DSL lines do not, and 5G depends on location.

## Fiber internet: fastest and most consistent

Fiber-optic internet sends data as pulses of light through strands of glass. Because light in fiber barely degrades over distance and isn't affected by electrical interference, fiber can support very high speeds and stays consistent even at peak hours.

### Where fiber shines

- **Symmetrical speeds.** Many fiber plans offer the same upload as download, which is a big deal for video calls, cloud backups and content creators. See [why upload speed matters](/blog/why-upload-speed-matters) for the practical impact.
- **Low latency.** Fiber typically has the lowest ping of any home connection, ideal for gaming and real-time work.
- **Future headroom.** Multi-gig plans (2, 5 or even 10 Gbps) are increasingly offered on fiber.

### Watch out for

Not all "fiber" is full fiber. In the UK and parts of Europe, **FTTC** (fiber to the cabinet) uses fiber to a street cabinet and copper for the last stretch, which is really a form of VDSL. **FTTP** or **FTTH** (fiber to the premises or home) is the full-fiber version. In the US, look for "fiber to the home" rather than "fiber-powered" marketing.

## Cable internet: fast downloads, slower uploads

Cable internet runs over the same coaxial lines used for cable TV, using a standard called **DOCSIS**. Modern DOCSIS 3.1 networks commonly deliver gigabit downloads, and newer upgrades are improving upload speeds.

### Strengths

Cable is widely available in urban and suburban areas, and download speeds can rival fiber. For a household that mostly streams, browses and games, a good cable plan is usually excellent.

### Weaknesses

- **Asymmetric speeds.** Upload is often only 10–50 Mbps even on fast plans, which can limit video calls with several people at once or large uploads.
- **Shared capacity.** Homes in a neighborhood share a node. At busy evening hours, speeds can drop if the node is congested.
- **Modem matters.** Using an old DOCSIS 3.0 modem on a fast plan can cap your speed. Check your provider's list of approved modems.

## 5G home internet: wireless and flexible

5G home internet (sometimes called fixed wireless access) uses a cellular gateway in your home that connects to a nearby mobile network tower, then shares the connection over WiFi or Ethernet.

### Strengths

- **Easy setup.** No technician or cable; you plug in the gateway and place it near a window.
- **Competitive pricing.** Plans are often simple, with no long contracts.
- **Good speeds with strong signal.** Many users see 100–300 Mbps downloads, which is enough for 4K streaming and remote work.

### Weaknesses

- **Variable performance.** Speed depends on signal strength, distance from the tower, weather, obstacles and how many people use the tower.
- **Higher, less stable latency.** Ping is usually higher than on fiber or cable and can spike, which matters for competitive gaming. Our guide to [a good ping for gaming](/blog/what-is-a-good-ping-for-gaming) explains the thresholds.
- **Network management.** Home internet customers can be deprioritized behind mobile phone users when a tower is busy.

### Tips to get the most from 5G home internet

1. Place the gateway near a window facing the nearest tower; the gateway's app often shows signal quality.
2. Test in several spots and at different times of day before settling on a location.
3. Connect your main computer or console by Ethernet to the gateway if possible.
4. Keep the gateway away from metal objects and thick walls.

## DSL internet: the legacy option

DSL (digital subscriber line) sends data over traditional copper telephone lines. **ADSL** typically tops out around 24 Mbps download, while **VDSL** can reach up to roughly 50–100 Mbps on short lines.

The biggest factor is **distance**. The farther your home is from the exchange or street cabinet, the weaker the signal and the slower the speed. Two houses on the same street can get very different results.

DSL's advantage is that it works almost anywhere there's a phone line, and latency is often reasonable. But upload speeds are low, and many providers in the US, UK and Europe are retiring copper networks in favor of fiber. If fiber, cable or a reliable 5G option is available, it will almost always be a significant upgrade.

## Which connection type is best for you?

### For gaming

Fiber first, cable second. Both offer low ping; fiber is usually more stable. 5G can be playable but is more likely to have lag spikes. Whatever you use, a wired connection from router to console helps.

### For working from home

Fiber, because symmetrical upload keeps video calls and file uploads smooth. Cable works well for most remote workers if the upload is at least 10–20 Mbps. See [internet speed for working from home](/blog/internet-speed-for-working-from-home) for detailed numbers.

### For streaming

Any connection that reliably delivers about 25 Mbps or more per 4K stream will do, so cable, fiber and good 5G connections are all fine. DSL may struggle with multiple simultaneous streams.

### For rural homes

Options may be limited to DSL, 5G or 4G home internet, fixed wireless or satellite. Test 5G coverage at your address first, since it often beats long-line DSL.

## How to check what you're really getting

Advertised speeds are "up to" figures. To see what your connection actually delivers:

1. Connect a computer to your router with an Ethernet cable to remove WiFi from the equation.
2. Pause downloads, streaming and backups on other devices.
3. [Run a free speed test](/) and note download, upload, ping and jitter.
4. Repeat at different times, including a weekday evening, to check peak-hour performance.
5. Compare results with your plan and with our guide to [what a good internet speed is](/guides/good-internet-speed).

If results are consistently far below your plan, contact your provider with your test results; it may be a line, modem or node issue.

## Bottom line

If fiber is available at your address, it's the best all-round choice for speed, upload and latency. Cable is a strong, widely available alternative, especially for download-heavy households. 5G home internet can be a great value where the signal is strong, while DSL is best treated as a fallback.

Before switching, compare the plans available at your address and [test your current speed](/) so you know exactly how much of an upgrade you'd be getting.
