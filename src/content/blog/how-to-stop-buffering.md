**Buffering happens when your streaming app can't download video as fast as it plays, so it pauses to catch up.** The most common causes are weak WiFi where the TV or device sits, other devices competing for bandwidth, and streaming hardware or apps that are struggling. Most cases can be fixed at home in minutes, starting with a simple speed test at the spot where you actually watch.

**Key takeaways**

- Test your speed at the TV's location, not next to the router. That's the number that matters.
- HD streaming typically needs around 5 Mbps per stream; 4K usually needs 15–25 Mbps.
- Wired connections and 5 GHz WiFi are the two biggest upgrades for buffering TVs.
- Restarting the streaming device and app solves a surprising share of buffering issues.
- If only one service buffers, the problem may be on its end, not yours.

## Why does streaming buffer?

Streaming apps download video a few seconds ahead of what you're watching and store it in a **buffer**. As long as data arrives faster than it plays, you never notice. When the incoming data slows down, the buffer drains, and the app pauses to refill it. That pause is the spinning wheel.

Modern services also use **adaptive bitrate streaming**: they automatically lower the picture quality when your connection slows, then raise it again when things improve. So buffering is really a sign that your connection dropped below even the lowest quality the app was willing to fall back to, or that it dropped suddenly and the app didn't adapt fast enough. Blurry, fluctuating picture quality is the early warning sign of the same problem.

## How much speed do you need to stream without buffering?

The figures below are typical recommendations from major streaming services. Treat them as minimums per stream, and add headroom for everyone else in your home.

| Video quality | Typical speed per stream | Comfortable with headroom |
|---|---|---|
| SD (480p) | 3 Mbps | 5 Mbps |
| HD (720p–1080p) | 5 Mbps | 10 Mbps |
| 4K Ultra HD | 15–25 Mbps | 30–40 Mbps |
| Live TV / sports in HD | 8–10 Mbps | 15–20 Mbps |

If two people stream 4K while someone else is on a video call, you can need 60 Mbps or more of steady capacity. For a full breakdown by service, see [how much internet speed you need for streaming](/blog/internet-speed-for-streaming-netflix-youtube).

## First, find out where the problem is

Before changing settings, spend two minutes diagnosing. It saves a lot of guesswork.

1. Take your phone or laptop and stand or sit exactly where the TV or streaming device is.
2. [Run a free speed test](/) and note download speed, ping, and jitter.
3. Repeat the test next to the router.
4. Compare the two results with the speeds in the table above.

If speed is fine near the router but poor at the TV, it's a **WiFi coverage problem**. If speed is poor everywhere, it's a **plan, ISP, or congestion problem**. If speed is fine at the TV but the stream still buffers, look at the **device, app, or service**. Our [WiFi speed test](/tools/wifi-speed-test) page has tips for testing different rooms consistently.

## 10 fixes to stop buffering

Work through these roughly in order. The early ones are quick and free; the later ones take more effort but solve stubborn problems.

### 1. Restart the streaming device and the app

Smart TVs and streaming sticks run for weeks without a real restart, and apps accumulate memory problems over time. Fully unplug the TV or stick for 30 seconds, not just standby, then reopen the app. This alone fixes a lot of intermittent buffering.

### 2. Restart your modem and router

Unplug both for 30 seconds, plug in the modem first, wait until it's fully online, then plug in the router. This clears overloaded connection tables and can get you a fresh connection from your ISP.

### 3. Pause other heavy traffic

Large game downloads, cloud photo backups, system updates, and file uploads compete with your stream. Uploads in particular can disrupt everything else on the connection. Pause them during movie night, or schedule them for overnight.

### 4. Lower the streaming quality temporarily

Most apps let you set playback quality manually. Dropping from 4K to 1080p cuts bandwidth needs dramatically. It's not a permanent fix, but it confirms whether bandwidth is the bottleneck: if buffering stops, you know you need a faster or steadier connection at that spot.

### 5. Switch the TV to 5 GHz WiFi

The 2.4 GHz band reaches farther but is slower and far more crowded, especially in apartments. If your router broadcasts separate networks, connect the TV to the 5 GHz one. If the TV is far from the router, 5 GHz may be weaker there, so test both. The shorter-range, faster band is usually the better choice for a TV in the same or next room.

### 6. Move the router or improve placement

Routers hidden in cabinets, behind TVs, or at floor level in a corner of the house lose a lot of signal. Place the router centrally, in the open, and raised off the floor. Even moving it a few feet out from behind furniture can make a measurable difference.

### 7. Connect the TV with an Ethernet cable

This is the single most reliable fix. A wired connection removes WiFi interference and signal loss entirely, and most smart TVs, game consoles, and many streaming boxes have an Ethernet port. If running a cable is impractical, a mesh node with an Ethernet port placed near the TV is a good compromise. See [Ethernet vs WiFi](/blog/ethernet-vs-wifi) for when wired is worth the effort.

### 8. Update the TV software and the streaming app

Manufacturers and services regularly fix playback bugs. Check for both a system update in the TV settings and an app update. On older smart TVs that no longer receive updates, an inexpensive external streaming stick often performs far better than the built-in apps.

### 9. Clear the app cache or reinstall the app

On Android TV, Google TV, and Fire TV devices, you can clear an app's cache in the settings. On other platforms, uninstalling and reinstalling the app does the same job. Corrupted cached data can cause stuttering and repeated buffering.

### 10. Check for ISP problems or upgrade your plan

If speeds are low even next to the router on a wired connection, the issue is upstream. Check your ISP's outage page, test at different times of day, and compare results with your plan's advertised speed. If evening speeds collapse but daytime is fine, you may be dealing with congestion or traffic management; our article on [whether your ISP is throttling your internet](/blog/is-my-isp-throttling-my-internet) walks through how to tell.

## When buffering isn't your fault

Sometimes the streaming service itself is the problem. Major live events, new season releases, and big sports broadcasts can overload a service's delivery network. If one app buffers while another streams 4K without issue at the same moment, your connection is very likely fine.

DNS and VPNs can also play a role. A VPN adds overhead and may route you to a distant server, which can trigger buffering on otherwise healthy connections. Try streaming with the VPN off to compare.

## How to keep buffering from coming back

Once things are working, a few habits keep them that way. Restart streaming devices every week or two, keep the router firmware updated, and schedule large downloads and backups for overnight. If your household has grown, with more people streaming, gaming, and working from home at once, check whether your plan still has enough headroom for everyone at peak times.

## Bottom line

Buffering is almost always a bandwidth or stability problem at the point where you watch, not a mystery. Measure speed at the TV, compare it with what your stream needs, and then work through restarts, competing traffic, WiFi band, router placement, and finally a wired connection.

Start with the diagnosis: [run a speed test from your couch](/) and next to the router, and you'll know within minutes which of the fixes above will make the spinning wheel disappear.
