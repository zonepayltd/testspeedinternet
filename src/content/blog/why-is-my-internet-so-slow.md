Your internet is usually slow for one of four reasons: a weak or crowded WiFi signal, outdated or overloaded equipment, a device that is busy or underpowered, or congestion and limits on your provider's side. The fastest way to narrow it down is to run a speed test on a computer plugged into the router with Ethernet. If that wired result is fine, the problem is inside your home; if it's slow too, look at the modem, the line, or your provider.

**Quick answer**

- Slow on WiFi but fast on Ethernet: it's your **wireless network** (distance, walls, interference, band).
- Slow on every device, even wired: check the **modem, router, and provider**.
- Slow on one device only: the **device** is the bottleneck.
- Slow mainly in the evening: likely **network congestion** in your area.
- Pages slow to start but fast once loading: consider **DNS or latency** issues.

## Start with a quick diagnosis

Before trying fixes at random, spend five minutes narrowing down where the slowdown lives. It saves a lot of guesswork.

1. [Run a speed test](/) on the device that feels slow and note download, upload, and ping.
2. Plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable and run the test again.
3. Compare the wired result with the speed in your plan.
4. Repeat the wired test in the evening if the problem seems to come and go.

For a more careful routine, see our step-by-step guide on [how to test internet speed accurately](/blog/how-to-test-internet-speed-accurately). Then use the causes below, grouped by where they occur.

## WiFi problems (causes 1–5)

When wired speed is fine but WiFi is slow, one of these is usually to blame.

### 1. Too far from the router

WiFi signal weakens quickly with distance, and speed drops along with it. A device two floors away may get a fraction of the speed you'd see in the same room. **Fix:** move closer to test, then consider relocating the router or adding a mesh node.

### 2. Walls, floors, and obstacles

Brick, concrete, metal, mirrors, and even fish tanks absorb or reflect WiFi. A router tucked inside a cabinet or behind a TV loses range. **Fix:** place the router high, central, and in the open. Our guide on [how to boost your WiFi signal](/blog/how-to-boost-wifi-signal) covers placement in detail.

### 3. Interference and crowded channels

In apartment buildings, dozens of networks may share the same 2.4 GHz channels. Microwaves, baby monitors, and some Bluetooth devices add noise too. **Fix:** use the 5 GHz or 6 GHz band where possible, or let the router pick a less crowded channel by restarting it.

### 4. Connected to the wrong band

Many devices cling to the 2.4 GHz band because it reaches farther, even when 5 GHz is available and much faster. **Fix:** if your router supports it, create separate network names for each band and connect nearby devices to 5 GHz. Keep 2.4 GHz for distant rooms and low-bandwidth smart devices.

### 5. A weak extender in the chain

Basic WiFi extenders often cut speed significantly because they use the same radio to talk to both the router and your device. **Fix:** move the extender to a spot halfway between router and dead zone, or switch to a mesh system or a wired access point.

## Router and modem problems (causes 6–9)

If your wired speed test is also slow, your equipment may be the bottleneck.

### 6. The router needs a restart

Routers are small computers. Over weeks of uptime, memory leaks and stuck connections can slow them down. **Fix:** unplug the modem and router for 30 seconds, plug the modem in first, wait for it to connect, then the router.

### 7. Outdated firmware

Router manufacturers release firmware updates for performance, stability, and security. **Fix:** log in to the router's admin page or app and install any available update. Enable automatic updates if offered.

### 8. Old or underpowered hardware

A router from many years ago may only support older WiFi standards and may not be able to route traffic at today's plan speeds. The same goes for older cable modems on fast plans. **Fix:** check that your modem is approved for your plan and consider a router with WiFi 6 or newer.

### 9. Too many connected devices

Smart speakers, cameras, TVs, phones, and laptops all compete for airtime. Budget routers can struggle with dozens of active clients. **Fix:** remove devices you don't use, put smart-home gadgets on a guest network, and upgrade the router if you have a busy household.

## Device problems (causes 10–12)

If only one phone or laptop is slow, look closely at that device.

### 10. Background downloads and sync

System updates, game downloads, and cloud backups can quietly use most of your bandwidth. **Fix:** check Task Manager on Windows (Performance and Processes tabs) or Activity Monitor on macOS (Network tab) to see what's using data, and pause it.

### 11. A VPN or security software

VPNs add encryption and route traffic through a distant server, which lowers speed and raises ping. Some antivirus web filters also slow browsing. **Fix:** temporarily disable them and retest. If speeds jump with the VPN off, try a closer VPN server or a lighter protocol.

### 12. Old WiFi hardware or malware

An older laptop's WiFi card may top out far below what your router can deliver. Malware can also consume bandwidth in the background. **Fix:** run a malware scan, update network drivers, and consider a USB WiFi adapter or Ethernet for older machines.

If you have worked through the causes so far, now is a good time to [run a free speed test](/) again and see whether anything has changed.

## Provider and line problems (causes 13–15)

When everything at home checks out and wired speeds are still slow, look outward.

### 13. Peak-time congestion

On shared networks, particularly cable, many neighbors draw on the same local capacity. Speeds may dip in the evening and recover late at night. **Fix:** test at different times to confirm, then raise it with your provider if the drop is severe and consistent.

### 14. Your plan is slower than you need

Sometimes nothing is broken; the household has simply outgrown the plan. Several 4K streams, gaming downloads, and video calls at once can saturate a modest connection. **Fix:** compare your usage with our guide to [what counts as a good internet speed](/guides/good-internet-speed) and consider an upgrade.

### 15. Line faults, outages, or throttling

Damaged cables, a loose coax connector, or a faulty line can cause slow or unstable speeds. Some providers also slow certain traffic or reduce speed after a data cap. **Fix:** check your provider's outage page, tighten connections, and see our guide on [whether your ISP is throttling you](/blog/is-my-isp-throttling-my-internet).

## Summary table: symptom, likely cause, first fix

| Symptom | Likely cause | First thing to try |
|---|---|---|
| Slow in far rooms only | Distance or obstacles | Move router to a central, high spot |
| Slow on WiFi, fast on Ethernet | WiFi band or interference | Switch to 5 GHz, restart router |
| Slow on one device | Device or background apps | Check Task Manager or Activity Monitor |
| Slow on everything, even wired | Modem, router, or line | Restart modem and router, check firmware |
| Slow mainly in the evening | Network congestion | Test at different times, contact provider |
| Fast downloads but laggy calls/games | High latency or bufferbloat | Run a [ping test](/tools/ping-test), enable QoS/SQM |

## When slow internet isn't about speed

Sometimes the speed test looks fine but the internet still feels sluggish. That usually points to latency rather than bandwidth. High ping, jitter, or packet loss makes web pages hesitate, video calls stutter, and games lag, even on a fast plan.

A common hidden cause is **bufferbloat**: when someone uploads or downloads heavily, the router's buffers fill up and every other packet waits in line. Enabling smart queue management (SQM) or QoS on your router, if available, often makes a dramatic difference. Slow DNS can also delay the start of each page load; switching to a faster DNS resolver can help in some cases.

## Bottom line

Slow internet feels like one problem, but it's usually one of a handful of specific causes. Testing on Ethernet first tells you whether to focus on your WiFi and devices or on your modem and provider, and most home fixes, such as moving the router, switching bands, restarting, or updating firmware, cost nothing.

Work through the list from the top, retesting after each change so you know what actually helped. When you are ready to check your progress, [test your internet speed](/) and compare it with your earlier results.
