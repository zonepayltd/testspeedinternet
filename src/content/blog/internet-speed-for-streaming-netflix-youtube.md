For a single stream, you need roughly **5 Mbps for HD** and **15–25 Mbps for 4K**, depending on the service. Netflix recommends about 15 Mbps for 4K, YouTube about 20 Mbps, and Disney+ about 25 Mbps. A household that streams on several screens at once should add those numbers together and leave headroom, which is why a plan of **50–100 Mbps** comfortably covers most families. The bigger risk is usually WiFi at the TV, not the plan itself.

**Quick answer**

- SD streaming: about 1–3 Mbps per stream.
- HD (720p–1080p): about 5 Mbps per stream.
- 4K Ultra HD: about 15–25 Mbps per stream, depending on the service.
- Multiple TVs: add up the streams, then add 25–50% headroom for other activity.
- Consistency matters more than peak speed; a steady 30 Mbps beats a connection that swings between 5 and 200.

## Recommended streaming speeds by service

Each major platform publishes its own guidance. The table below reflects the commonly cited minimum recommendations from each service's help pages. Services adjust these over time as compression improves, so treat them as typical targets rather than fixed rules.

| Service | SD | HD (720p/1080p) | 4K Ultra HD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Netflix | ~3 Mbps | ~5 Mbps | ~15 Mbps |
| YouTube | ~1 Mbps (480p) | ~2.5 Mbps (720p), ~5 Mbps (1080p) | ~20 Mbps |
| Disney+ | — | ~5 Mbps | ~25 Mbps |
| Hulu | — | ~3 Mbps (on-demand) | ~16 Mbps |

These are figures for a single stream. They also assume the speed is sustained; short dips below these levels can trigger quality drops or buffering.

### Why the 4K numbers differ

Each service uses different video codecs, bitrates and quality settings. Newer codecs such as HEVC, VP9 and AV1 compress video more efficiently, so a modern TV or streaming stick can often deliver 4K at a lower bitrate than older hardware. The higher recommendations, like Disney+'s 25 Mbps, give extra margin so the picture stays sharp during complex, fast-moving scenes.

### Live TV and sports

Live streams, such as live sports or live TV services, often need slightly more bandwidth and are more sensitive to interruptions because there's less buffer to fall back on. If you watch a lot of live sport, add extra headroom and prioritize a stable connection to the TV.

### Your plan tier and device also limit quality

Speed alone doesn't unlock 4K. Most services only stream in Ultra HD if your subscription tier includes it, the title is available in 4K, and your TV or streaming device supports the required format and copy protection (HDCP 2.2 over HDMI for many external devices). Web browsers on computers are often limited to lower resolutions on some services as well. If you have plenty of bandwidth but never see 4K, check your plan, the device's supported formats and the app's playback settings before blaming your connection.

### Adaptive streaming explained

Streaming apps use **adaptive bitrate** technology: they measure your connection every few seconds and switch to a higher or lower quality version of the video to avoid stopping. That's why a picture may start blurry and sharpen after a few seconds, or soften briefly when someone else starts a big download.

## How much speed does a whole household need?

The real question is rarely "how fast for one Netflix stream?" It's "how fast for everything happening at once?" Here's a practical way to estimate it.

1. Count the maximum number of screens that stream at the same time, such as the living room TV, a bedroom TV and a tablet.
2. Assign each a quality level: about 25 Mbps for 4K, 5 Mbps for HD, 3 Mbps for SD.
3. Add other regular activities: about 3–4 Mbps per video call, a few Mbps for music and browsing, and more for game downloads.
4. Add 25–50% headroom for background updates, cloud backups and peaks.

| Household scenario | Estimated need | Comfortable plan |
|---|---|---|
| One person, HD on a laptop or TV | ~5–10 Mbps | 25 Mbps+ |
| Couple, one 4K TV plus phones | ~30 Mbps | 50 Mbps+ |
| Family, two 4K TVs, HD tablet, video call | ~65 Mbps | 100 Mbps+ |
| Large household, 3–4 4K streams, gaming, remote work | ~120 Mbps+ | 200–300 Mbps+ |

## How to check if your connection is fast enough for streaming

Testing next to the router tells you what your plan delivers; testing at the TV tells you what streaming actually gets. Do both.

1. **Test the plan:** connect a laptop to the router with Ethernet and [run a free speed test](/). This is your baseline.
2. **Test at the TV:** take a phone or laptop to where the TV sits and test again over WiFi. Our [WiFi speed test](/tools/wifi-speed-test) works well for this.
3. **Use the TV's own test:** many smart TVs and streaming devices include a network test in their settings. The Netflix app also has a built-in diagnostic under its help or settings menu.
4. **Test at peak time:** run a test on a weekday evening, when both your home and your provider's network are busiest.
5. **Check ping and jitter:** for on-demand streaming these matter less, but high jitter or packet loss can still disrupt live streams. Learn more in [what jitter and packet loss are](/blog/what-is-jitter-and-packet-loss).

If the baseline is fine but the TV location is slow, the problem is your WiFi, not your plan.

## Why you might buffer even with a fast plan

A fast plan doesn't guarantee smooth streaming. Common culprits include:

- **Weak WiFi at the TV.** TVs are often in corners, behind cabinets or far from the router. Wall-mounted TVs may also have small antennas.
- **2.4 GHz connections.** If the TV connects to the slower 2.4 GHz band, speeds may drop below 4K requirements. See [2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz WiFi](/blog/2-4-ghz-vs-5-ghz-wifi).
- **Network congestion at home.** Large game downloads, cloud backups or someone uploading video can crowd out your stream.
- **Older streaming hardware.** Early smart TVs and old streaming sticks may lack modern codecs or have slow WiFi chips.
- **Provider or peering congestion.** Occasionally the slowdown is between your ISP and the streaming service, especially at peak hours.

For step-by-step fixes, read our guide on [how to stop buffering](/blog/how-to-stop-buffering).

## Ways to make streaming more reliable

You don't always need a faster plan. These changes often have a bigger effect:

- **Use Ethernet for the main TV.** A wired connection removes WiFi as a variable entirely. If running a cable isn't practical, powerline adapters or a mesh node near the TV can help.
- **Move the router or add a mesh point.** Even a few meters and one fewer wall can make a large difference.
- **Use the 5 GHz band** for TVs and streaming devices with a decent signal.
- **Schedule big downloads.** Set game consoles and computers to download updates overnight.
- **Lower the quality on secondary screens.** A phone or tablet doesn't need 4K; most apps let you set a data-saving or lower-quality option.

## Data usage and caps

If your plan has a monthly data cap, streaming quality matters for your bill as well as your speed. As a rough guide, SD streaming uses under 1 GB per hour, HD about 1–3 GB per hour, and 4K roughly 7 GB per hour or more. A household watching four hours of 4K a day could use around 800 GB or more in a month from streaming alone.

Most streaming apps let you choose a default quality per profile, which is a simple way to stay under a cap. 5G home internet and some cable plans are more likely to have caps or management policies, so check your terms.

## Bottom line

For a single screen, 5 Mbps covers HD and 15–25 Mbps covers 4K on Netflix, YouTube and Disney+. For a household, add up your simultaneous streams and leave headroom; around 100 Mbps is plenty for most families. If you still see buffering, focus on the connection between the router and the TV.

To see whether your connection is up to the job, [test your internet speed](/) both next to the router and from where you actually watch.
